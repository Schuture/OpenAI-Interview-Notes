## Problem

This is the first round of the process: a 30-minute phone call with a recruiter, not an engineer. It
combines a standard set of behavioral questions with an introduction to the logistics of the rest of the
process — timeline, compensation expectations, and work location. The questions fall into four groups.

### Background

- Walk through your background, your resume, and your most recent project.
- Of everything you've worked on, which one are you proudest of, and why?
- Describe a project that was technically difficult, and what made it hard.

### Motivation and direction

- Why do you want to join OpenAI specifically, rather than another company?
- Which kind of work do you prefer: product-facing, infrastructure, or research?
- Why are you leaving your current position?

### Leadership and conflict

- Have you led other people before? Describe how.
- Tell me about a conflict that came up on a project and how you handled it.

### Process and logistics

- What are your salary expectations?
- When could you start if you were offered the role?
- What other interview processes are you in, and what stage are they at?
- What is your preferred work location, and do you need visa sponsorship?
- Is there anyone who could speak to your work — a reference, or someone you know at the company?

## Reference solution

<summary>Show the reference solution</summary>

The person on the other end of this call is evaluating fit and gathering logistics, not technical depth
— keep every answer shorter and less technical than you would in a later round, and stay consistent with
what you say to whoever runs the rounds after this one.

### Background

What it probes: whether you can summarize your own experience concisely for a listener who won't follow
technical detail, and connect it to the role you're applying for.

Skeleton for the walkthrough: what you do now, in one sentence → two achievements most relevant to this
role, each with a number → one sentence on why you're looking at this opportunity now. Aim for about two
minutes; anything longer starts eating into the rest of the call.

For "proudest of" and "technically difficult", reuse one of the same two achievements rather than
introducing a third project — pick the harder of the two, state the difficulty in one or two sentences
(what made it hard, not how you solved it step by step), and leave the full technical walkthrough for a
round built for it.

Most common way to lose points: narrating your resume chronologically, job by job, instead of leading
with what's relevant to this role; or describing the work in terms only an engineer on the same team
would follow — "we moved the pipeline off the legacy scheduler onto DAG-based orchestration" — instead of
naming the outcome. Fix: pick the two most relevant achievements before the call and state each as an
outcome a non-engineer can follow, in time, people affected, or money — "the change cut a recurring
multi-hour delay to under ten minutes" — not as an internal implementation detail.

### Motivation and direction

What it probes: whether your interest in OpenAI is specific rather than generic, and whether you've
actually thought about what kind of work you want to do here.

Skeleton: one sentence connecting something specific about OpenAI's work to your own trajectory — a
product, a research direction, a technical problem, not "AI is the future" — a stated preference among
product-facing, infrastructure, and research work, with one reason, and a reason for leaving your
current role framed around what you're moving toward rather than only what's wrong with where you are.

Most common way to lose points: an answer generic enough to fit any AI company, or a reason for leaving
that reads as complaining about your current employer. Fix: name something concrete about the work
itself rather than the company's reputation, and give the reason for leaving only as what you want to
do next, without running down where you are now.

### Leadership and conflict

What it probes: whether you can describe your own role in a leadership or conflict situation concretely,
rather than describing what the team accomplished in general.

Skeleton: the situation in one sentence, the specific action you took, and the outcome. For leadership,
state the scope plainly — how many people, what kind of authority, since a tech lead directing peers is a
different answer from a formal manager — rather than letting the title imply more than it was. For
conflict, state what the actual disagreement was in one sentence, what you did to resolve it, and the
outcome, including anything you'd still do differently.

Most common way to lose points: a "we" answer that never states your own action, or a conflict story
that isn't really a conflict — a technical decision you made alone, described as if someone had opposed
it. Fix: put yourself in the sentence — "I proposed X, then a teammate pushed back on Z, so we agreed to
Y instead" — and pick a story with a real, stated disagreement.

### Process and logistics

What this group probes is fit and scheduling, not a single correct answer — the recruiter needs your
range and timeline to coordinate the rest of the loop.

**Salary expectations.** Skeleton: if you don't yet know the level and the compensation structure for
the role — the mix of base, equity, and bonus, and how levels map to it — ask about that first; it is
also fair to ask what range the role is budgeted at. Once you know, give a range rather than a point
number, and say whether it refers to base pay or to total compensation. The range should come from
market research for the role and level — public compensation data, recruiters, people in comparable
roles — not from your current salary. Most common way to lose points: stating a single number with no
basis, or anchoring to your current pay — both hand the other side a number to negotiate down from
before you know whether it fits the level you're actually being considered for. Fix: research a market
range beforehand for the roles and levels you're plausibly targeting, and lead with a question about
level and structure if that hasn't come up yet.

**Availability.** State your real timeline, including a standard notice period, plainly. Overstating your
availability to sound eager creates a scheduling problem later that costs more credibility than a
realistic number would have.

**Other processes.** State honestly which stage you're at and your rough overall timeline; there's no
need to name the companies or share confidential details from those processes — the stage and the
timeline are what's needed to coordinate scheduling. If another process has given you a decision
deadline, say the date, so that scheduling can take it into account.

**Location and visa.** State your preferred location(s), how flexible you are, and your visa or
work-authorization status plainly and early if it could affect the timeline — raising it later costs more
time than raising it now.

**References.** Name only people who know your work first-hand and whom you have already asked. If you
haven't asked them yet, say that you'll send names once you have, and then do it promptly. The same goes
for people you know inside the company: mention a contact only if they would actually vouch for your
work.

### Questions worth asking

This call is also your chance to gather information for the rest of the process. Worth asking before you
hang up:

- What format and length are the remaining rounds, and how many are there?
- Roughly how much time is there between rounds?
- What level is the role scoped at?
- Is the role tied to a specific team, or is the team decided later in the process — and if later, when?
- What should you prepare or bring to the next round?

When you follow up after any later round, the useful question is the same kind: what the next step is
and roughly when to expect it.
